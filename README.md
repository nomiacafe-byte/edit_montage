# Rafa Studio

Private creative editing workspace for Rafa Pizza.

## Product promise
Turn long, unordered user-shot footage into a deliberate, premium 20–30 second food/restaurant Reel. Behave like a specialist editor and creative director: every selected moment and every cut must have an editorial reason.

## Non-negotiable creative rules
- Use only footage supplied by Rafa. Never generate synthetic video shots.
- Accept long recordings (including ~20-minute videos) and many short clips in arbitrary order.
- Target runtime: 20–30 seconds maximum; shorter if the story is stronger.
- No random trimming and no fixed-duration chopping. Cuts must follow action boundaries, visual changes, narrative logic, emotional beats or intentional audio rhythm.
- Preserve a consistent Rafa visual signature while adapting each story to the actual footage.
- Premium, cinematic food-commercial finish: tactile detail, natural movement, precise cuts, restrained transitions and typography.
- Do not promise virality. Optimize testable creative hypotheses and learn from actual performance.

## Cross-event intelligence and visual continuity
Rafa Studio must be able to create one coherent film from footage captured at different events, dates, venues, cameras and shot types. It must not assume that adjacent clips belong to the same event, nor treat chronological order as the default story.

### Build a story across different sources
1. **Understand each clip independently:** identify the visible subject, action, event context clues, shot size, camera angle, camera movement, lighting, dominant colors, people/food/environment and technical quality.
2. **Build a searchable visual inventory:** tag moments by action and semantic role (dough preparation, topping, oven, reveal, serving, guest interaction, atmosphere, setup, etc.), plus source event when it can be inferred. Keep uncertain event assignments explicitly uncertain.
3. **Find narrative relationships:** connect clips through shared action, matching shapes or movement, ingredient continuity, cause-and-effect, visual contrast, recurring motifs, sound or a clear emotional progression.
4. **Plan transitions before cutting:** decide why shot B should follow shot A. Examples: a hand movement can continue across two different events; a raw dough shot can lead into a finished pizza; oven heat can bridge into a lively service scene. A transition must feel intentional, not merely possible.
5. **Control continuity:** compare screen direction, movement direction, eyelines where relevant, shot scale, camera height, color temperature, exposure, white balance, background clutter and action phase. Use a motivated contrast when exact matching is impossible.
6. **Manage event changes honestly:** if a recap spans multiple events, use visual or narrative bridges (repeated actions, details, sound, rhythm, brief establishing shots) so the viewer experiences one deliberate montage. Do not create a false impression that two moments happened in the same event when that would mislead.
7. **Protect pacing and variety:** avoid consecutive shots with the same composition, repeated action or identical visual energy unless repetition is a deliberate rhythmic device. Alternate detail, medium and wide shots when the available footage supports it.
8. **Run a continuity review:** inspect every adjacent pair and the whole sequence at normal speed. Flag abrupt lighting/location/wardrobe/prop changes, mismatched movement, incomplete gestures, confusing chronology and shots that weaken the emotional arc.

### Transition relationship scoring
For each proposed adjacent pair, estimate and retain separate signals:
- semantic/story connection;
- action or movement match;
- composition and shape match;
- color/light compatibility;
- camera direction and screen direction;
- audio/beat compatibility;
- temporal/event consistency;
- contrast value when the edit intentionally changes energy or setting.

Do not collapse these into a single opaque score. A strong creative contrast can be better than a technically perfect match. The editor must explain the intended relationship in plain language and mark low-confidence transitions for review.

### Two editing modes
- **Single-event story:** preserve the event's natural progression when useful, from arrival/setup through preparation, cooking, service and guest experience.
- **Multi-event brand recap:** build a thematic progression across events (craft, energy, hospitality, abundance, signature pizzas) rather than stitching together unrelated highlights. The sequence should feel like one film about Rafa Pizza, not a random slideshow of past jobs.

## Signature story arc: one Reel, one clear journey

The default Rafa Pizza Reel should feel like a short, complete story with a visible beginning, progression and payoff. The viewer should understand the journey without needing captions to explain disconnected images.

### Preferred narrative progression
This is a flexible editorial backbone, not a rigid shot checklist. Use the best available footage and adapt the order when the story benefits:
1. **Hook:** open on a compelling sensory or human moment that creates curiosity (a finished pizza reveal, a dramatic dough stretch, a lively guest reaction, a pizza entering/leaving the oven). The hook may tease a later payoff.
2. **The craft begins:** show Rafa and the team stretching dough. Establish hands, technique, movement and the human work behind the food.
3. **The pizza takes shape:** move naturally into sauce, ingredients and topping. Make the transformation legible; use close-ups and action matches so the viewer feels the pizza progressing.
4. **The experience comes alive:** weave in guests, service and atmosphere at a point where they deepen the story. Customer shots should feel connected to the food and hospitality, not randomly inserted as filler.
5. **Heat and transformation:** show the oven, baking, finishing gestures or the final reveal. This should feel like the consequence of the preparation viewers have just watched.
6. **Payoff and emotional close:** deliver the finished pizza, a slice, a shared moment or a memorable team/guest image. End on warmth, appetite, energy and the Rafa identity.

The edit can start with the payoff and then travel back to the beginning, but it must return to a satisfying resolution. It may intercut people and process when the cut has a clear relationship (for example, a guest arriving while a pizza is being finished). Never shuffle stages arbitrarily.

### Narrative logic and viewer emotion
- Define one sentence describing what this Reel is about before selecting the sequence.
- Give every shot a narrative role: hook, establish, craft, transform, human connection, anticipation, payoff or close.
- Build cause-and-effect where possible: dough is stretched, toppings are added, the pizza is baked, it is served and enjoyed.
- Build anticipation by withholding the full reveal just long enough to make the viewer want it, without confusing them.
- Let positive emotion rise through visible teamwork, confidence, generosity, lively service, delicious food and genuine guest moments. Do not manufacture reactions or stage false event continuity.
- Use human moments to give meaning to the craft; use food moments to reward the human story.
- Maintain a coherent energy curve: curiosity → immersion → rising energy → sensory reward → warm memorable finish.
- A beautiful shot that interrupts the story should be moved, shortened or removed. Story clarity outranks the number of attractive shots included.

### Rafa brand feeling
The film should communicate the Rafa Pizza identity through the footage and its treatment, not rely on a logo slapped at the end. Aim for a recognizable blend of:
- creative, confident pizza craft;
- warm, welcoming hospitality;
- lively, convivial event energy;
- premium but unpretentious presentation;
- tactile food imagery and human connection.

Use the established Rafa visual language consistently (including its burgundy and warm cream/beige palette where graphic elements are appropriate), while preserving the natural colors of the food and locations. Avoid heavy filters that make different events look artificially identical.

### Storyboard test before rendering
Before rendering, the editor must be able to answer:
- What is the story in one sentence?
- What question or promise does the opening hook create?
- Can a first-time viewer follow the order of preparation and experience?
- Does each transition connect by action, meaning, emotion, sound or intentional contrast?
- Do the guest moments enrich the process rather than interrupt it?
- Does the oven/finalization sequence pay off what the opening promised?
- Does the ending leave a positive feeling and a clear impression of Rafa Pizza?

If the sequence fails these questions, rebuild the order before polishing effects or color.

## Long-footage intelligence pipeline
1. **Ingest and inspect:** read duration, frame rate, resolution, orientation, audio tracks and codec; create proxy/thumbnail assets without altering originals.
2. **Detect structure:** identify shot/scene boundaries, camera movement, changes in framing, silence/speech/music and meaningful action phases.
3. **Understand content:** sample frames over time and inspect promising intervals more densely; classify pizza, dough, ingredients, hands, oven, plating, guests, environment and other visible subjects/actions. Transcribe speech when present.
4. **Find usable moments:** detect action starts, peak moments and natural endings (e.g. dough stretch reaches full extension, pizza enters/leaves oven, knife completes a cut, slice lifts, cheese pull peaks). Preserve handles before/after the action.
5. **Score candidates:** combine visual clarity/focus, exposure, composition, motion, uniqueness, sensory appeal, story relevance, action completeness and technical quality. A score is a ranking aid, not the editor's only decision.
6. **Build narrative:** choose a hook, context/promise, escalating process, payoff and close. Ensure continuity and variety in shot size, angle, subject and motion.
7. **Place edits precisely:** cut on meaningful action boundaries or visual/audio beats when they improve the story. Avoid cutting mid-gesture, repeating nearly identical shots, dead time and transitions that distract.
8. **Render and review:** create a low-resolution preview first, inspect pacing and continuity, then render high-quality MP4 using source-appropriate resolution and frame rate.

## Editorial decision model
For every chosen segment, retain:
- source filename and in/out timecodes;
- what is visible and what action occurs;
- why the moment is selected;
- its role (hook, setup, build, transition, payoff, close);
- technical confidence and any quality caveat;
- for cross-event edits, the reason it follows the previous shot and the continuity strategy used.

For every rejected candidate, retain a short reason when useful (blur, duplicate action, weak framing, no complete action, irrelevant to story). This makes decisions explainable and lets Rafa correct the system.

## Masterpiece quality gate
A draft is not ready merely because it contains attractive shots. Before export, evaluate it as a complete film:
- **Story:** one clear idea, purposeful progression, satisfying payoff and a memorable ending.
- **Editing:** every cut has a reason; no accidental jump, dead beat, arbitrary speed ramp or decorative transition.
- **Continuity:** adjacent shots connect or create a deliberate, legible contrast; cross-event changes never feel like mistakes.
- **Rhythm:** pacing varies with the action and emotional energy; music supports rather than dictates the edit.
- **Image:** focus, exposure, color and crop are consistent enough for a polished finish without making source footage look artificial.
- **Sound:** retain useful natural sounds (dough, oven, slicing, room atmosphere) where they add tactility; balance them with music and speech.
- **Brand:** premium, warm, confident and recognizably Rafa; no generic social-template aesthetic.
- **Truth:** no misleading event continuity, fabricated moments or synthetic footage.
- **Platform delivery:** vertical framing and export settings are checked; text remains legible and within safe areas.

The system should produce an edit decision list and a preview first. It must identify unresolved weak points rather than silently hiding them. Rafa can approve, reject or request a revision before the final render.

## Reference learning: compare to real successful Reels
Maintain a curated reference library of public food/restaurant Reels with:
- source URL, creator, capture date and visible performance context where available;
- opening hook type, first-frame composition and time-to-payoff;
- narrative beats, shot duration distribution, framing changes and audio strategy;
- format archetype (sensory process, transformation, tutorial, reveal, behind-the-scenes, event story, etc.);
- why the structure may work and how to adapt the principle to Rafa without copying footage, script or distinctive creative expression.

Compare Rafa's draft to references structurally, not by blindly imitating them. Separate verified platform guidance from third-party sample analyses and editorial hypotheses. Recheck current platform guidance periodically.

## Psychology and performance loop
Design each Reel around:
- **Attention:** immediate visual curiosity or a compelling question.
- **Comprehension:** viewers quickly understand what they are watching.
- **Anticipation:** a process or transformation creates a reason to stay.
- **Sensory reward:** texture, sound, heat, stretch, crispness, cut or reveal.
- **Social value:** a moment people might send to a friend or use to start a conversation.

Measure after publication:
- average watch time and retention/completion;
- likes per reach;
- sends/shares per reach;
- saves, profile visits and booking enquiries as business outcomes.

Store the creative hypothesis, hook, runtime, edit decisions and Instagram Insights for each published Reel. Compare against Rafa's own baseline and identify repeatable wins. Do not infer that views alone prove a format is successful.

## Creative framework (flexible starting hypothesis)
- 0–2s: hook (strongest visual or intriguing moment).
- 2–6s: promise/context.
- 6–17s: process and escalation (hands, dough, ingredients, oven, detail).
- 17–24s: payoff (finished pizza, slice, guest moment).
- 24–30s: memorable close and subtle booking cue only when appropriate.

This is a flexible grammar, not a mandatory formula. A video may open with the payoff and then reveal how it happened.


## Batch import and privacy-first processing

### User workflow
- The user must be able to select many video files in one file-picker operation (target: 30–100+ clips) or drag and drop a whole batch.
- Do not impose an arbitrary low file-count cap. Show the number of selected clips and their combined size, reject non-video files gracefully, and avoid adding exact duplicate selections twice.
- Read lightweight metadata with bounded concurrency; do not decode every full-resolution source into memory just to build the library view.
- Support common phone-camera formats, especially MOV and MP4. Report unsupported/corrupt files clearly rather than silently ignoring them.
- Keep the upload/import flow simple: select once, review the batch, start one analysis job.

### Temporary files only — no permanent footage library
Rafa Studio is a production tool, not an archive. The intended lifecycle is:
1. Source clips are selected for one editing project.
2. Uploads, proxies, thumbnails, extracted audio and analysis artifacts exist only in temporary project storage.
3. The editor analyses the footage, prepares the edit decision list, renders the final MP4 and offers it for download.
4. After the MP4 is safely delivered, delete the source uploads and all derived temporary assets.
5. Also purge temporary assets on cancellation, unrecoverable failure and abandoned-project expiry. Use a short, explicit TTL as a safety net.
6. Keep no reusable footage archive and no permanent copies of source videos. Retain only the final MP4 on the user's device unless the user explicitly chooses otherwise.
7. Do not claim deletion until the storage layer confirms it. Provide a clear “delete project and all source files” action and visible cleanup status.

### Important implementation/security requirements
- Use private, access-controlled temporary object storage; never make source clips public.
- Upload large files with resumable/multipart transfer and per-file progress so a dropped connection does not force the entire batch to restart.
- Queue analysis/render jobs; do not hold a browser request open for the entire edit.
- Enforce file type/size validation server-side as well as client-side, and protect against archive bombs, malformed media and resource exhaustion.
- Use short-lived signed upload/download URLs, isolate each project, and delete temporary credentials when jobs finish.
- Store only the minimum project/job metadata needed to complete and report the render. Avoid retaining source footage or extracted media after cleanup.
- The final MP4 should be downloaded by the user before cleanup is finalized; if delivery fails, allow a short retry window without retaining the source files indefinitely.

**Current status:** the front end includes batch import, one-click generation, resumable chunk upload, job progress, MP4 preview and download integration. The backend includes a real FFmpeg render pipeline and multi-moment selection for long recordings. If `OPENAI_API_KEY` is configured on the server, sampled low-resolution still frames (not source videos) are sent to the OpenAI Responses API for semantic labels such as dough, topping, guests, oven and finished pizza. It uses capture timestamps where available or a low-confidence brightness estimate as a chronology fallback. The Render backend is source code with automated container/smoke-test workflows, but it is **not yet deployed or connected to the public site**. Full event grouping, action-phase boundaries, audio-aware edits and an intelligent narrative/transition planner remain unfinished. Do not describe the current version as a finished elite editor.


## One-click generation, preview and download
The intended end-user flow must be simple and explicit:
1. Select or drop the full batch of source videos.
2. Review the imported clips and their upload status.
3. Press one primary **“Générer la vidéo”** button to launch the complete creative pipeline.
4. Show meaningful progress stages while the job runs (upload, media inspection, moment selection, story assembly, continuity review, preview render, final render).
5. Produce a 20–30 second vertical Reel (shorter when the footage supports a stronger story).
6. Present an in-page playable preview of the generated edit before final download.
7. Let the user approve the preview, request a revision, or download the final MP4.
8. After successful delivery/download, purge all source footage and temporary derivatives according to the privacy lifecycle above.

The generation button must not be a fake/demo action: it should be enabled only when the batch is valid and the real processing service is available. The UI must communicate queueing, progress, recoverable errors and completion clearly. A long-running edit must continue as a background job if the user navigates away or the mobile browser temporarily loses focus; reconnecting should restore the job status.

### Chronology and lighting continuity — mandatory
Rafa Studio must infer the likely real-world time progression across the selected footage, especially when multiple clips come from one event:
- Estimate relative time-of-day from exposure, ambient light, window/daylight, artificial lighting, sky/background cues and visual changes across clips. Use capture timestamps and file metadata when trustworthy, but never assume filenames or picker order are chronological.
- Build a confidence-rated chronology graph for clips and candidate moments. Distinguish strong evidence (reliable capture timestamps or clear continuous visual progression) from weaker visual estimates.
- **Do not create an accidental time reversal.** If the event visibly progresses from daylight to dusk to night, the edit must not jump from night back to bright daylight later in the story.
- When chronology is uncertain, prefer an order that avoids obvious light reversals, or use an intentional thematic/multi-event structure only when the change is visually legible and not misleading.
- Do not force all footage into one chronology if it comes from different events/dates. First infer event groups; preserve temporal progression within each group, then bridge groups intentionally.
- Treat lighting continuity as a story constraint, not merely a color-grading problem. Color correction may gently harmonize exposure/white balance, but must never disguise a real passage of time or make night footage look like day.
- Before rendering, run an explicit chronology audit over the proposed shot order. Flag any daylight/dusk/night reversal and either reorder the shots or explain why the contrast is intentional.
- If the system cannot confidently determine the order, flag the uncertainty for review instead of inventing a precise timeline.

### Preview and final delivery
- Render a lightweight review preview first, with the actual proposed shot order, trims, transitions, crop and sound treatment.
- Preview must be playable directly in Rafa Studio, with clear **“Télécharger le MP4”** and revision/approval controls.
- The final MP4 is rendered only after the preview passes the quality gate or the user approves it. The preview must be representative of the final edit, not a different storyboard mock-up.
- Preserve a safe delivery window: do not delete source material until the final MP4 is available to download and delivery has been confirmed; then clean up source and derivative files, with a short retry window if download fails.

## Current implementation
`index.html` provides batch import and the generation/preview/download interface. `engine-client.js` manages chunked uploads, job polling, preview playback and final MP4 delivery acknowledgement. `backend/app.py` provides the temporary project API, sampled-frame analysis, optional vision classification, FFmpeg rendering and cleanup. The API URL remains intentionally blank in `config.js` until the backend is deployed and its health endpoint is verified.
